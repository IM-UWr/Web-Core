Theoretical mathematics

Organised, in-depth knowledge of key mathematical theories — from algebra and analysis, through topology and logic, to probability — and fluency in rigorous proofs.

About

Theoretical mathematics is a specialisation offered at both bachelor’s and master’s level for those who want to get to know mathematics from the inside — to understand how its key theories are built and why its theorems are true. Its graduates have organised, in-depth knowledge of key mathematical theories and are fluent in carrying out rigorous proofs. The core of the specialisation is category T — a closed list of core elective courses designed to give you broad knowledge of many different areas of mathematics. That is why a high level of difficulty is not the main criterion for including a course on the list, and the number of courses from each area is deliberately limited.

Most courses consist of a lecture with exercise classes and end with an exam. Key courses are taken in their extended (R) versions, which present complete theory with full proofs of most results and spend more time on harder problems. Alongside lectures you learn to work on your own: in Proseminar 1 you prepare talks on advanced topics, in the Individual project you write a paper similar to a diploma thesis, and at master’s level you take part in survey seminars on recent achievements and key directions of development in mathematics and in the master’s seminar.

Category T covers the areas in which the Institute’s departments do research: Algebra and Model Theory, Geometry, Set Theory and Topology, Functional Analysis, Mathematical Analysis, Differential Equations and Probability Theory. The Institute’s research projects come from these fields — for example “Arithmetic and geometry of fields with operators”, “Geometry and topology of Artin groups”, “Analytic P-ideals, Banach spaces and measure algebras” and “Continuous and discrete perspectives in harmonic analysis” (see Grants). The Institute’s research seminars are open to everyone interested, and thesis topics offered by staff (examples below) concern these areas.

Unlike data analysis, actuarial and financial mathematics and applied mathematics, your plan has no courses such as Databases, Linear models or Deterministic modelling; instead it contains a closed list of theoretical courses, compulsory extended versions of key courses and your own written paper already at bachelor’s level.

Bachelor’s level

In semesters 1–4 the plan is close to those of the other specialisations, but some courses must be taken in their extended (R) versions: Topology R (semester 2), Measure and integration R (semester 3), and Probability 1 R and Differential equations 1 R (semester 4). In addition you take Proseminar 1 (semester 3) and Analytic functions R (semester 4). You do not have to take Introduction to combinatorics in semester 1. In semesters 5–6 you take Functional analysis 1, Differentiable manifolds and 4 courses from category T, and in the Individual project you prepare, under the guidance of an academic of your choice, a paper similar in character to a diploma thesis. The bachelor’s degree ends with a written exam common to the whole programme (without a thesis).

Master’s level

You declare the specialisation at the start of semester 1. The plan includes 6 courses from category T, two survey seminars, mathematics courses from categories M or T (at least 42 ECTS) and L, M or T (at least 8 ECTS), as well as courses common to the whole programme (including master’s seminars and English). You complete your studies with a master’s thesis and a diploma exam.

Category T

In USOSweb, category T is marked with the code T and is a subcategory of category M; in the Institute’s timetable, T courses appear on one list together with the other M courses. Courses are divided into three groups: A and B are offered regularly, every year or every two years, while group C courses are run irregularly, at the request of lecturers or students. The schedule given in the course list reflects past practice and is not a commitment — the offer may change depending on student interest and the availability of lecturers.

Previous curriculum (students who started in 2022/23–2025/26)

If you started your studies between 2022/23 and 2025/26, the previous curriculum applies to you. The category T rules are the same: 4 courses at bachelor’s level and 6 at master’s level. Under the previous bachelor’s curriculum, students of this specialisation take Measure and integration and Topology instead of Analysis and topology R, and the Team specialisation project in semester 5. From 2026/27 the old course Topology is offered as Topology R, and Measure and integration offered in 2026/27 corresponds to the new Measure and integration R.

Who is it for

For those who want to understand how mathematical theories are built, enjoy getting to the heart of a proof, and are thinking about advanced master’s studies and later research work — including at a university. The specialisation requires the extended (R) versions of several key courses, so be ready for a more demanding programme.

How the specialisation works

  1. Semester 1 Starting on the common plan Everyone takes the same courses. If you already declare that you plan theoretical mathematics, you may drop Introduction to combinatorics. The extended versions of Mathematical analysis, Linear algebra and Introduction to mathematics are strongly recommended.
  2. Semesters 2–4 Extended versions and first talks Topology R (semester 2), Measure and integration R and Proseminar 1 (semester 3), and in semester 4 Probability 1 R, Differential equations 1 R and Analytic functions R.
  3. End of semester 4 Declaring the specialisation You declare your specialisation by 30 June using Form C2.
  4. Semesters 5–6 Specialisation courses Functional analysis 1, Differentiable manifolds, 4 courses from category T, the Team project (semester 5) and the Individual project (semester 6).
  5. After semester 6 Bachelor's exam A written diploma exam common to the programme (5 ECTS) covering Mathematical analysis 1–3, Linear algebra 1–2, Algebra 1, Differential equations 1 and Probability 1. You do not write a diploma thesis.
  6. Master's — semester 1 Declaration at master's level You declare the specialisation by 15 October using Form C3. You need background from 6 of 8 bachelor's courses (see the list in the FAQ below).
  7. Master's — semesters 1–4 Category T courses and seminars 6 category T courses, two survey seminars, courses from categories M or T (at least 42 ECTS) and L, M or T (at least 8 ECTS), and in semester 2 Master's seminar 1, which helps you choose your thesis topic.
  8. Master's — semesters 3–4 Master's thesis and exam You register your supervisor and thesis topic at the start of semester 3 using Form E. You work on the thesis for two semesters and finish with the diploma exam (written and oral part). The thesis is worth 12 ECTS and the exam 4 ECTS.

What you will learn

Specialisation courses — bachelor's (curriculum from 2026/27)

  • Topology RSemester 2. Topology of metric spaces and general topology: completeness, compactness, the Baire theorem, different kinds of convergence of sequences of functions — a preparation for functional analysis and probability theory, among others.
  • Measure and integration RSemester 3. Constructing measures (including Lebesgue measure), measurable functions, the integral and limit theorems, product measures and the Fubini theorem.
  • Proseminar 1Semester 3. You learn to explore and analyse advanced mathematical topics on your own and present them in talks.
  • Probability 1 R and Differential equations 1 RSemester 4 — courses common to the programme; in this specialisation the extended version is compulsory. Sums of independent random variables, the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem; ordinary differential equations and elements of partial differential equations.
  • Analytic functions RSemester 4. Functions of a complex variable: the complex derivative, the Cauchy theorem and integral formula, Taylor and Laurent series, singular points and evaluating integrals.
  • Functional analysis 1Semester 5. An introduction to Banach and Hilbert spaces — from the Hahn–Banach theorem to bounded linear operators.
  • Differentiable manifoldsSemester 6. Smooth manifolds, the tangent bundle, vector fields and their flows — the language needed in differential geometry and topology, Lie group theory and dynamical systems, among others.
  • Individual projectSemester 6. Under the guidance of an academic of your choice you prepare a paper similar to a diploma thesis, learning how to write mathematical papers and the basics of research work.
  • 4 core elective courses from category TAccording to the plan, 2 in each of semesters 5 and 6 (see the list below).
  • Mathematics electives from categories L, M or TAt least 6 ECTS.

Specialisation courses — master's (curriculum from 2026/27)

  • 6 core elective courses from category T36 ECTS (see the list below).
  • Survey seminars (2 courses)Recent achievements and key directions of development in selected areas of mathematics, and the art of oral presentation; 60 hours in total, 4 ECTS.
  • Mathematics courses from categories M or TAt least 42 ECTS.
  • Mathematics courses from categories L, M or TAt least 8 ECTS.
  • Master's seminar 1 and 2Semester 2 — the mathematician's toolkit, choosing a topic and the rules of thesis writing; semester 4 — presenting topics related to your master's thesis (in this specialisation in the format set by the supervisor).

Category T — group A

  • Algebra 2 RField extensions and the basics of Galois theory, finite fields and modules, i.e. linear algebra over rings. (Summer semester, every 2 years.)
  • Functional analysis 2Operator theory on Banach and Hilbert spaces — the spectrum, self-adjoint, positive and compact operators, convergence of operators. (Summer semester, every year.)
  • Descriptive set theoryDefinable sets in Polish spaces — Borel, analytic and co-analytic sets — the Suslin scheme, separation and selection theorems. (Summer semester, every 2 years.)
  • Logic RThe logical foundations of mathematics — classical logic and Gödel's model existence theorem, elements of model theory, decidability and undecidability of theories. (Winter semester, every 2 years.)
  • Probability 2 RConditional expectation, martingales and stopping times — the foundations of stochastic analysis and the theory of Lévy processes, among others. (Winter semester, every year.)
  • Differential equations 2 RModern methods for ordinary and partial differential equations — qualitative properties of solutions, the phase flow and the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem, calculus of variations. (Winter semester, every year.)
  • Algebraic topology 1The fundamental group and homology groups of topological spaces — how to compute them and use them in other areas of mathematics. (Every 2 years.)

Category T — group B (other regularly offered courses)

  • Stochastic analysisBrownian motion, Gaussian and Lévy processes, and the basic methods and theorems of stochastic analysis. (Winter semester, every year.)
  • Formal languages and computational complexityFormal language theory, decidability and undecidability, and computational complexity; taught by the Institute of Computer Science. (Summer semester, every year.)
  • Combinatorial group theoryFree groups and free products, group presentations, group actions on trees and Bass–Serre theory. (Every 2 years.)
  • Nonlinear functional analysisFixed-point theorems (Brouwer, Schauder, Leray–Schauder), Sobolev spaces and their applications to partial differential equations. (Summer semester, every 2 years.)
  • Model theoryTypes, stability theory and Morley's categoricity theorem, and applications of model theory in algebra. (Summer semester, every 2 years.)
  • StatisticsClassical and modern mathematical statistics — point and interval estimation, hypothesis testing, asymptotic properties. (Winter semester, every year.)
  • Fourier series and the Fourier transformTrigonometric series and the Fourier transform, and their applications in approximation theory and in solving differential equations. (Summer semester, every year.)

Category T — group C (courses offered irregularly)

  • Axiomatic set theoryMethods of building models of set theory, above all the method of forcing.
  • Commutative algebraRings, ideals and modules, Noetherian rings, dimension and Gröbner bases.
  • Banach algebras and spectral theorySpectral analysis of normal operators, the basics of Banach algebras and unbounded self-adjoint operators.
  • Algebraic number theoryFinite extensions of the field of rational numbers and their arithmetic.
  • Analytic number theoryNumber theory using analytic methods — estimates, series, integrals and analytic functions.
  • Algebraic geometryThe basic notions of algebraic geometry — an algebraic view of geometric objects and its applications in other areas of mathematics.
  • Differential geometryCurves and surfaces, differential forms and the Stokes theorem, connections, Riemannian manifolds and the Gauss–Bonnet theorem.
  • Lie groups and Lie algebrasAn introduction to Lie groups and Lie algebras and the classical structure theorems.
  • Topological groupsMetrisability of topological groups, subgroups and quotients, Haar measure on locally compact groups, Polish groups.
  • Mathematical foundations of quantum mechanicsThe mathematical formalism of quantum mechanics based on Hilbert spaces and the probabilistic interpretation of the wave function.
  • Measures on topological spacesGeometric and topological properties of measures, weak convergence of probability measures, non-measurable sets and measures on topological groups.
  • Operator semigroups and evolution equationsThe theory of linear operator semigroups applied to models of evolution phenomena in physics and biology, with generalisations to nonlinear problems.
  • Graph theoryThe structure of graphs, matchings, cycles and paths, Ramsey theory, planar graphs, and probabilistic and algebraic methods.
  • Group representation theoryRepresentations and characters of groups, Schur's lemma, induced representations and applications in quantum mechanics, among others. Formerly Introduction to group representation theory.
  • Dynamical systemsThe key notions of dynamical systems and the methods of ergodic theory — from classical mechanics to chaotic systems.

Skills

  • You will have organised, in-depth knowledge of key mathematical theories and understand how they are built.
  • You will carry out rigorous proofs with confidence and judge which assumptions of a theorem are essential.
  • You will find an example that illustrates a notion, or a counterexample that refutes a false conjecture or an unjustified argument.
  • You will get to know many areas of mathematics — from algebra and analysis, through logic, set theory and topology, to probability and differential equations.
  • You will be familiar with recent achievements and key directions of development in selected areas of mathematics.
  • You will formulate and test simple research hypotheses, using or adapting the methods you have learnt.
  • Under a supervisor's guidance you will work out a mathematical topic on your own — in the individual project and later in your master's thesis.
  • You will prepare a talk on an advanced topic, including for listeners from outside the field, and lead a discussion about it.
  • You will use mathematical literature in English with ease (at level B2+ at master's level).
  • You will be prepared to take up research work, including at a doctoral school.

Careers

Theoretical mathematics is above all a path for future researchers. The curriculum (UWr Senate resolution no. 48/2026) describes it as follows:

  • after the bachelor’s degree you are prepared to start advanced master’s studies and, in the longer term, to take up research; the specialisation lets you develop analytical and research skills early on and prepares you for further development within academia;
  • after the master’s degree you have in-depth knowledge of how mathematical theories are built and of the latest research trends, and you are prepared to take up research — including the challenges of a doctoral school.

The specialisation prepares people for research and teaching at universities. Its graduates form the talent pool that keeps up the standard of higher education and the development of science.

The natural next step is the Doctoral School. Among the Institute’s research projects there are also projects in theoretical mathematics led by people holding a master’s degree, e.g. “Boundaries of Helly groups” and “Free and iterative operators on fields” (see Grants).

FAQ

When and how do I declare this specialisation?

At bachelor’s level you make the final choice at the end of semester 4 — submit the declaration by 30 June using Form C2. In semesters 1–4 the plans of data analysis, actuarial and financial mathematics, applied mathematics and theoretical mathematics are similar enough that you can wait with the decision, but it is worth preparing for it from semester 2 (especially when choosing course versions). At master’s level you submit the declaration at the start of semester 1, by 15 October, using Form C3. Forms and dates: Deadlines and forms.

Can I change my specialisation later or take two?

At bachelor’s level you may change your declaration until the end of your studies, although this may require making up differences in the programme. In exceptional cases, with the Dean’s consent, you can choose two specialisations. At master’s level, changing your declaration or taking two specialisations requires the Dean’s consent.

Do I have to choose the extended (R) versions, and do I take Introduction to combinatorics?

In this specialisation the extended versions of Topology R, Measure and integration R, Probability 1 R and Differential equations 1 R are compulsory. The extended versions of Mathematical analysis I–III, Linear algebra 1R–2R, Introduction to mathematics R and Algebra 1 R are strongly recommended — completing the specialisation without them is difficult, though possible.

Introduction to combinatorics (semester 1) is not required in this specialisation. Everyone is enrolled at first, but if you declare that you plan theoretical mathematics, you may drop it. If you eventually choose another specialisation, you can make it up in semester 3 or 5; if you pass it and later choose theoretical mathematics, it counts towards the electives from categories L, M or T.

Which courses should I plan ahead?

Category T courses have prerequisites, so take them into account when planning. For example: Descriptive and Axiomatic set theory require Introduction to set theory (available in semester 2 or 4 as a continuation of Introduction to mathematics R); Stochastic analysis requires Probability 2 R; Model theory requires Logic R and Algebra 1; Algebraic topology 1 requires Algebra 1 and Topology R; Differential geometry requires Differentiable manifolds; Functional analysis 2 requires Functional analysis 1 and Topology. The full list of prerequisites is in the curriculum guides.

I have a bachelor's degree in another specialisation or from another university. Can I choose this specialisation at master's level?

Yes, if you have the knowledge and skills from any 6 of these 8 bachelor’s courses in mathematics: Topology R, Differential equations 1 R, Algebra 1 R, Differentiable manifolds, Probability 1 R, Measure and integration R, Analytic functions R, Functional analysis 1. Gaps can be filled during your studies, but ECTS credits for supplementary courses do not count towards the 120 ECTS required for the degree.

Can a course outside the T list, or from another university, count as a category T course?

The category T list is closed — requests (from students or lecturers) to add new courses are not accepted. If you complete part of your studies at another university, a course passed there may be recognised as equivalent to a course on the T list after comparing the syllabuses. The Dean decides after consulting the specialisation coordinator, so it is worth getting the coordinator’s preliminary approval before submitting a formal request.

How can I propose that a group C course be run?

Group C courses are run irregularly — at the request of a lecturer or of students, provided enough people sign up. Student requests for the next academic year go to the specialisation coordinator by 31 May (teaching for the whole following year is assigned in June).

What does the master's exam look like in this specialisation?

The exam has a written and an oral part. According to the specialisation rules, the written part consists of 4 problems, 2 of which concern two group A courses of your choice — so it is worth passing at least two group A courses during your studies. You choose the problem areas when registering for the exam. The oral part concerns the topic of your master’s thesis. Details: Exams and diploma.
